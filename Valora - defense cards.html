Jude — Slides 1–3 (the story, trend & structure) · easiest

Methods 1–2 · horizontal and vertical analysis

Revenue change −4.6% / −11.3% / −23.1%
HOW(this year − last year) ÷ last year, from Exhibit 2.
WHYHorizontal analysis: a percentage shows the decline is persistent and speeding up, which a raw amount hides.
Equity −211 (insolvent)
HOWIt's the 2026 balance in Exhibit 3; we checked it by roll-forward: 2025 equity 7,715 minus the 2026 loss 7,926 = −211.
WHYNegative equity means liabilities exceed assets — the clearest statement of insolvency.
Distributions 1,860 vs profit 1,663
HOWAdded owner distributions 2024+2025 (1,120 + 740) and compared with net income (948 + 715).
WHYShows the family took out more than the company earned, draining the cash buffer before the crisis.
Reclassified gross margin 62% (not 88%)
HOWMoved the 715 of production cost in Exhibit 9 out of operating expenses into cost of sales, then gross profit ÷ revenue.
WHYReported 88% only looks high because cost of sales is materials only; 62% is comparable to a normal producer. EBIT is unchanged, so it's a fair reclassification, not a manipulation.
Common-size shares (opex 125.6%, equity −5.0% of assets)
HOWEach income line ÷ revenue; each balance line ÷ total assets.
WHYVertical analysis removes size, so you can see the cost base didn't shrink with sales and the financing structure flipped negative.

Alexander — Slides 4–6 (ratios and DuPont)

Method 3–4 · liquidity, profitability, efficiency, leverage

Current 0.59 & quick 0.21
HOWCurrent = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Quick = (cash + receivables) ÷ current liabilities, dropping inventory.
WHYTwo standard liquidity tests; the quick ratio is stricter, and with zero cash it's our strongest distress signal.
Operating margin −38% (ex-impairment)
HOWEBIT ÷ revenue, using EBIT with the 7,026 impairment stripped out.
WHYTo show operations lost money on their own, before any one-off write-down — a fairer read of the business.
Cash conversion cycle −284 days / DPO 896
HOWDIO + DSO − DPO, each = 365 ÷ turnover. Payables turnover uses a purchases proxy: cost of sales + inventory change.
WHYA negative cycle usually means strength, but here it's distress — suppliers unpaid. We use the purchases proxy because the case doesn't report credit purchases and tells us to.
Average balances (opening + closing) ÷ 2
HOWFor any ratio mixing a flow and a stock; we use 2023 closing balances for the 2024 averages.
WHYThe Guide requires averages so the ratio matches the period; 2023 data exists, so no proxy is needed.
ROE −211% & DuPont
HOWNet margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier, which reconciles exactly to net income ÷ average equity.
WHYDuPont shows what drives ROE. We flag 2026 as not meaningful because negative equity makes the average an artefact — we report it but don't interpret it, as the case instructs.

Alan — Slides 7–9 (cash flow, the 2026 monthly crisis, the forecast)

Method 5 and start of 6

2025 operating cash flow 1,345, of which +355 working capital
HOWFrom Exhibit 4; the 355 splits into +636 unpaid suppliers and accruals, less 281 tied up in receivables and inventory.
WHYTo show the 2025 cash was borrowed from suppliers, not earned by operations.
Monthly overdraft path, ending 476
HOWRebuilt each month: collections (40% of last month's sales + 60% of the month before) minus supplier payments (Exhibit 7 schedule) minus cash costs; the overdraft fills any shortfall, interest 9% per year on the opening balance.
WHYThe case gives only the January opening and December closing, so we needed the path to see when distress began. It's right because it lands exactly on the case's year-end overdraft 476, payables 480 and interest 106.
January collection assumption
HOWReceivables at end-2025 (206) equal December sales, so we treat November as already collected.
WHYIt's forced by the data, not chosen: the alternative gives a year-end overdraft of 475.994, which misses the case's 476.465. Only our reading ties.
Forecast net income 242, equity 1,410, and the 39% cost cut
HOWBuilt the three statements from the Exhibit 10 assumptions. The 39% is 2026 cash costs 2,395 versus the 2027 requirement 1,466.
WHYBalance-sheet and cash checks are both zero, so the forecast is internally consistent. We state the cut like-for-like because the 74% target includes depreciation and the 2026 figure doesn't.

Vilhelm — Slides 10–12 (scenarios, DCF, conclusion)

Rest of Method 6, and Method 7

Sensitivity: margin ±30 vs growth ±8
HOWChanged one 2027 assumption at a time, holding the others fixed, and read the effect on net income.
WHYThat's the Exhibit 12 design; it isolates which single assumption matters most — here, gross margin, not sales volume.
Adverse scenario: EBIT 0, loss 268
HOWChanged all the linked adverse assumptions together (Exhibit 11), never mixing columns.
WHYA scenario is a coherent world, so related assumptions must move together — that's the difference from a sensitivity test.
WACC 9.94% on 2025 book weights
HOWWeighted 11.25% cost of equity (risk-free + beta × premium) and 4.875% after-tax cost of debt, using 2025 equity 7,715 and debt 2,000.
WHYValora is private (no market value) and 2026 equity is negative, so 2025 book weights are the teaching proxy the case tells us to use. We flag it likely understates the true cost of capital.
Equity value 3,939 vs book −211
HOWEnterprise value 6,216 minus debt 2,276 plus cash 0. Enterprise value = discounted FCFF plus a terminal value that is 83% of the total.
WHYDCF value is economic (future cash flows); book equity is accounting. The gap is the value of the plan, and it only exists if the plan is delivered — which is our whole conclusion.
